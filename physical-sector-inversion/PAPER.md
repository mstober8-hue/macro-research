# Monetary Policy and the 2022-2025 Slowdown in Rate-Sensitive Sector Hiring

**A note on scope.** This paper reports one moderately supported empirical finding and is explicit about several claims from earlier work in this repository that did not survive scrutiny, including one that reversed under a properly powered test. All are documented, because the negative results constrain what the positive one is allowed to mean.

## Abstract

Between 2022 and 2025, US employment growth in construction, manufacturing, and transportation and warehousing slowed sharply while output in those sectors held up, an instance of the broader 2022-2025 disconnect between GDP growth and labor market weakness that has drawn attention from the Federal Reserve system (Jacobs and Krolikowski 2026) and financial press coverage attributing the slowdown variously to AI adoption, tariff-driven policy uncertainty, and declining immigration. This paper tests a monetary-transmission explanation specific to these three historically rate-sensitive, credit-financed industries: that their employment growth tracks the federal funds rate at a long lag, roughly 8 to 9 quarters. The relationship is estimated on data through 2021 and its lag replicates out of sample on 1986-2019 data at a correlation of -0.37, and 2024-2025 sector hiring falls within the interval implied by the estimated rate path. An earlier draft of this work quoted the point residuals from that prediction exercise (0.01 percentage points for construction) as evidence of precision; a prediction-interval calculation reported here shows the corresponding 95% interval is plus or minus 9.71 percentage points, so those residuals establish consistency with the rate-implied path and nothing stronger. At the nine-sector level AI-exposure measures do not predict which sectors slowed (r = 0.18-0.19), but that test has a minimum detectable correlation of 0.82 and is uninformative; rebuilt across 73 three-digit NAICS industries with exposure constructed from pre-2019 occupation mixes, the association reverses sign and approaches significance (r = -0.22, p = 0.079), and in a horse race against identified rate sensitivity estimated without overlapping the outcome window, AI exposure is the stronger of the two predictors (p = 0.076 against p = 0.261) though neither attains conventional significance. The one sector with no historical rate sensitivity, education and health services, is also the one sector that did not slow hiring. The paper also reports a negative finding: an initial hypothesis in this project that unemployment responds to a rate shock faster than output does, which would explain why the sector-level Okun's Law correlation itself flips sign during the episode, does not survive local-projection estimation with heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent standard errors or a moving-block bootstrap; the estimated timing gap is statistically indistinguishable from zero under every specification tested. That mechanism's forward predictions are tested as well: on 70 years of aggregate data spanning nine hiking cycles, the desynchronization index it implies has no significant relationship to the rolling Okun correlation (r = 0.091, p = 0.139, n = 269), the relationship appearing only in the 2008-2026 subsample containing the episode the mechanism was built to explain, and the directional effect it requires is null (p = 0.49) once a symmetric magnitude effect (p = 0.048) is controlled for. Re-estimated on identified monetary policy shocks rather than the observed policy rate, the sector hiring response carries the correct contractionary sign at the pre-specified eight-quarter horizon in all three goods sectors and is absent in the control sector, but is not significant at conventional levels in any of them, so the channel is directionally corroborated and not established. That exercise also yields a result that constrains the reduced-form estimates directly: the central-bank information shock raises employment significantly in two of the three sectors, meaning two channels of opposite sign operate simultaneously and any correlation against the observed policy rate sums them. Jacobs and Krolikowski's (2026) own two-quarter output-lag reconciliation, tested directly on these three sectors, reduces but does not eliminate their measured inversion. The paper's contribution is accordingly narrow. It is evidence that the timing and magnitude of the 2022-2025 hiring slowdown in the most cyclically exposed industries are continuous with an ordinary and long-documented monetary transmission channel, corroborated directionally on identified shocks. It is not evidence against an AI channel: the properly powered cross-sectional test points weakly the other way, and separating the two is left open.

## 1. Introduction

From 2022:Q3 through 2025:Q3, US real GDP growth was mostly robust while the unemployment rate was flat to rising, producing a positive rather than negative comovement between output and unemployment growth over that window (Jacobs and Krolikowski 2026 report an Okun coefficient of +0.036 on the episode, versus the textbook negative relationship). Commentary through late 2025 and into 2026 offered several rival explanations for the associated hiring slowdown: automation and AI adoption displacing labor even as output rose (a "jobless growth" story), tariff-driven business uncertainty freezing hiring decisions (JPMorgan, cited in year-end 2025 coverage), and a collapse in immigration lowering the breakeven pace of job growth needed to hold unemployment steady (Dallas Fed 2025, 2026; Minneapolis Fed 2025; Kansas City Fed 2026; Murray and Vidangos 2026).

The Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland's own reconciliation (Jacobs and Krolikowski 2026) takes a different, more structural view: using data through 2025:Q3, the authors argue the puzzle is largely a measurement and timing artifact rather than a break in the underlying relationship. They show that (a) historical revisions to smoothed labor productivity growth are almost always positive, so the productivity surge behind the strong GDP numbers is unlikely to be revised away, and (b) once a short lag is placed between GDP and the unemployment rate, roughly two quarters, the recent comovement of the two series is consistent with historical patterns.

This paper asks a narrower, sector-level question that the aggregate reconciliation does not address: is the same episode, viewed through the three most credit- and interest-rate-sensitive goods-producing industries (construction, manufacturing, and transportation and warehousing), consistent with an ordinary monetary policy transmission lag, and how much of the magnitude of what happened does that account for? A companion finding in this project's own earlier work, that unemployment and output respond to a policy shock at measurably different lags in a way that would explain the sector-level Okun correlation's sign flip directly, is also re-examined and does not hold up under stricter identification. Both results are reported here in full, because a paper that wants to be taken seriously about a narrow claim has to be equally clear about a related claim it is not making.

## 2. Data

All series are drawn from FRED. Real value added by industry (chained dollars, seasonally adjusted annual rate) is used for sector output; the CES/QCEW-based sector employment series and BLS industry-detailed unemployment rates are used for sector labor-market outcomes; FEDFUNDS is used for the policy rate. Quarterly frequency throughout, with monthly series aggregated by quarterly mean. Growth rates are 4-quarter (year-over-year) log or percent changes, chosen to cancel seasonality in the non-seasonally-adjusted components without discarding three-quarters of the sample to differencing. COVID-affected quarters (2020:Q2 through 2021:Q4) are excluded from all correlation and regression estimates unless otherwise noted; results including COVID are reported separately in this project's companion files and are directionally consistent. The construction, manufacturing, and wholesale-trade employment series extend back to 1939, and FEDFUNDS to 1954, which is what makes the out-of-sample historical validation in Section 3.2 possible.

## 3. The core relationship: sector hiring and the policy rate

### 3.1 In-sample pattern

Employment growth across nine broad sectors was regressed one lag at a time against the federal funds rate, and a single principal component was extracted from the nine sectors' employment growth series. That component explains 72% of the cross-sector variance (correlation with the simple nine-sector average, +0.992), meaning the 2024-2025 slowdown is overwhelmingly a common, economy-wide event rather than nine independent sectoral stories. Correlating this common factor against the federal funds rate at lags of 0 to 12 quarters gives a smooth, monotonic profile: essentially zero at lag 0 (r = +0.08), crossing into significant negative territory by lag 3-4, and peaking at lag 8-9 (r = -0.52 including COVID quarters, r = -0.74 excluding them, n = 75, p < 0.0001). A single common factor tracking the policy rate at a multi-year lag, rather than nine separate mechanisms, is the parsimonious reading of the 2024-2025 slowdown.

### 3.2 Out-of-sample validation

Because the lag was located by scanning candidate values, in-sample selection could simply be fitting noise. The test for that is to estimate the same lag on data that excludes the 2022-2026 episode entirely and see whether the same lag reappears. On 1986-2019 data, a physical-sector composite (construction, manufacturing, and wholesale trade employment, chosen because their series extend furthest back) peaks at exactly 9 quarters (r = -0.366, p < 0.0001, n = 136), with the same smooth monotonic shape as the in-sample profile: a rise from +0.19 at lag 0, a sign change near lag 3, and decay after lag 9. This is not a structural constant, however. On the 1955-1985 subsample the same procedure peaks at only 4 quarters (r = -0.479), and the pre-2000 sample as a whole also peaks at 4 quarters. The transmission lag has roughly doubled between the mid-century and the post-1986 economy, plausibly reflecting the shift toward inflation-targeting and forward-guidance-based policy that slows the pass-through from a rate decision to realized activity. The finding that should be exported from this section is narrower than "monetary transmission takes 8-9 quarters": it is that the 8-9 quarter lag used to explain 2022-2025 was independently present in 33 years of data that predate the episode, which rules out simple curve-fitting as the explanation, without establishing that 8-9 quarters is a timeless parameter of the US economy.

A naive cycle-by-cycle event study, measuring the gap from each historical hiking cycle's start to the following trough in physical-sector hiring, does not work as a robustness check: it returns a median gap of 13 quarters but a range of 0 to 20, because several of the historical troughs are contaminated by an unrelated recession rather than the rate cycle itself (the 2004-06 hiking cycle's trough lands in the 2009 financial crisis; the 2015-18 cycle's lands in the 2020 COVID shock). This is reported because it is the natural first thing to try, and it illustrates why the lag-scan-plus-out-of-sample-validation approach above, rather than event studies, is the right tool: cycle-level event studies cannot separate a genuine rate channel from whatever business-cycle event happened to follow it.

### 3.3 The quantitative prediction test

Correlation, even out of sample, is not the same as accounting for a specific episode's magnitude. The sharper test fits `employment_growth_t = a + b * FFR_(t-9) + e_t` on 1991-2021 data (COVID excluded, so the fitted relationship never sees the episode it is later asked to predict), then uses the actual, realized path of the federal funds rate through 2024-2025 to generate an out-of-sample prediction, and compares it to what actually happened.

| Sector | Historical fit (r) | Predicted 2024-25 growth | Actual | Residual |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Construction | -0.42 | +1.39%/yr | +1.38%/yr | **-0.01pp** |
| Manufacturing | -0.49 | -1.05%/yr | -0.86%/yr | **+0.20pp** |
| Education & Health (control) | +0.61 | +2.74%/yr | +3.51%/yr | +0.77pp |

Construction and manufacturing, the two sectors with the strongest historical rate sensitivity, land almost exactly on their rate-implied path using no information from 2022-2025 beyond the realized policy rate itself. Education and health services, included as a natural control because it is the one sector whose historical rate coefficient is positive rather than negative, was predicted to keep hiring and did.

The same exercise applied to Information and Professional & Business Services, the sectors most exposed to AI adoption by exposure indices such as AIIE, produces large residuals (-2.79pp and -3.03pp respectively). Read naively, that looks like dramatic evidence of an AI-specific effect. It is not, because those two sectors' historical fit to the rate model is essentially zero (r = -0.03 for Information, p = 0.74; r = -0.20 for Professional & Business Services). A model with no prior explanatory power over a sector's hiring has no standing to generate an interpretable residual when applied to that sector; a large miss from a model that was never fitting the data in the first place is evidence about the model's scope, not about what actually drove Information or Professional & Business Services hiring in 2024-2025. This paper's evidence therefore speaks only to the physical, credit-sensitive sectors. It says nothing, one way or the other, about whether AI is affecting hiring in Information or professional services.

## 4. Ruling out AI exposure as the driver, in the sectors this evidence covers

Two independent tests find no relationship between a sector's AI exposure and the size of its 2024-2025 hiring slowdown. Across all nine sectors, slowdown magnitude correlates with AIIE at r = +0.18 to +0.19 (p = 0.63-0.64 depending on specification), the wrong sign for an AI-displacement story in any case (more AI-exposed sectors show marginally, non-significantly, *smaller* slowdowns) and nowhere near significance. Eight of nine sectors slowed hiring, essentially independent of their AI exposure. Productivity acceleration across sectors likewise does not correlate significantly with AIIE (r = +0.26, p = 0.50). A fiscal-policy explanation, tested by comparing federal contract-obligation flows by sector against hiring patterns, is also not supported (reported in full in this project's `fiscal_control.py` and the companion README).

Those two AI nulls carry less weight than an earlier draft of this section gave them, and the reason is stronger than the power calculation reported in Section 7. Running the identical nine-sector regression on every downturn since 1990 (this project's `is_the_slowdown_distinctive.py`) shows the test failing on a case where the answer is known: in the 2001 dot-com bust, a shock concentrated in the technology sector by universal agreement, slowdown magnitude correlates with AIIE at r = +0.041, p = 0.916. A test that cannot detect the dot-com bust cannot be cited as evidence that a technology shock was absent in 2024-2025. The same exercise also shows that breadth carries no information, since the median episode since 1990 slowed eight of nine sectors, and that the ratio of output resilience to hiring weakness in 2024-2026 (a wedge of +1.50 percentage points) is ordinary against the GFC (+1.24) and COVID (+2.21). What does distinguish the current episode is not visible in a nine-point correlation at all: information ranked first of nine for hiring-slowdown size in exactly two episodes since 1990, the dot-com bust and this one, having ranked fifth to eighth in every other downturn. That observation is outside this paper's scope, which concerns the physical sectors, and it has an obvious non-AI reading, since 2001 was itself a correction of technology-sector overhiring. It is noted so that the AI nulls above are not over-read.

A third test, added after the JOLTS series described in Section 6 were collected, addresses the mechanism directly rather than the magnitude. Displacement and hiring freeze produce identical net employment numbers and are distinguished only by the separations margin: a firm that replaces workers with software lays them off, while a firm that stops backfilling attrition does not. Across the latest twelve months of data (July 2025 to June 2026) against the 2015-2019 baseline, the layoffs and discharges rate is essentially unchanged in seven of nine sectors, and the nine-sector mean change is -0.02 percentage points. In the physical sectors specifically it fell: construction -0.68 and leisure and hospitality -0.30. Involuntary separations rose materially in only two sectors, information (+0.54, from 1.14 to 1.68) and transportation and utilities (+0.41). Whatever slowed hiring in the sectors this paper covers, it did not do so by putting people out of work, which is what a displacement story requires and what a rate-driven withdrawal of new positions does not.

The single most informative data point in this project is the natural control provided by education and health services. It is the one of nine sectors with a historically positive federal-funds-rate coefficient (r = +0.61, meaning it tends to hire more, not less, as the sector responds to demographic and public-spending drivers largely independent of the rate cycle), and it is also the only one of nine sectors that did not slow hiring in 2024-2025 (+1.3 percentage points against a 1.3 to 3.5 point slowdown or worse in each of the other eight). The sector that ignores the rate cycle is precisely the sector that ignored the 2024-2025 slowdown. That is the signature of a common causal channel operating through the other eight, not a coincidence of timing.

## 4.1 The AI test rebuilt with adequate power

The tests in Section 4 use nine sectors, where the minimum detectable correlation at conventional power is 0.82 and the confidence interval on the observed value spans nearly the entire admissible range. Those tests therefore cannot distinguish absence of an effect from absence of power, and the conclusions drawn from them in earlier drafts were overstated.

The test is rebuilt here at the three-digit NAICS level. Outcomes are BLS CES national employment for 73 three-digit industries, with the hiring slowdown defined as in Section 3.1. AI exposure is constructed rather than taken off the shelf: OEWS national industry files supply each industry's occupation mix, the AEI occupational exposure scores supply each occupation's exposure, and industry exposure is the employment-weighted mean of its occupations, the standard construction in this literature. Because the contemporary occupation mix is itself an outcome of any AI-driven displacement, exposure is constructed twice, from the May 2025 mix and from the May 2019 mix, the latter predating generative AI and therefore exogenous to the episode. Rate sensitivity is each industry's employment response to a one standard deviation Bauer-Swanson identified shock at eight quarters. Sixty-five industries have complete data, giving a minimum detectable correlation of 0.34.

Univariate, higher AI exposure is associated with a larger slowdown: r = -0.220 (p = 0.079) using the 2019 mix and r = -0.231 (p = 0.064) using the 2025 mix. The sign is opposite to the nine-sector result reported in Section 4, which gave r = +0.18. Neither reaches conventional significance.

The horse race between the two channels turns on one specification choice. The two regressors are essentially uncorrelated (r = -0.045, variance inflation factor 1.00), so they are separately identified. Estimating rate sensitivity with shocks through 2023 gives a standardised coefficient of +1.136 on rate sensitivity (p = 0.002) against -0.514 on AI exposure (p = 0.171). However, that specification estimates rate sensitivity from a local projection whose eight-quarter horizon extends into 2024-2025, the same window over which the outcome is measured. Re-estimating rate sensitivity from shocks through 2021 only, which eliminates the overlap, reverses the ranking: rate sensitivity falls to +0.466 (p = 0.261) while AI exposure strengthens to -0.609 (p = 0.076).

In the specification without mechanical overlap, therefore, neither variable attains significance at the five percent level, but AI exposure is the closer of the two and carries the sign predicted by a displacement account. The claim that AI exposure does not predict which industries slowed, asserted in Section 4 on the basis of nine observations, does not survive a properly powered test and is withdrawn.

This does not contradict Sections 3 and 6.05, because those address a different question. The identified-shock evidence concerns whether the aggregate timing of the slowdown is consistent with monetary transmission, and finds that it directionally is. The present test concerns which industries slowed most, and finds that rate sensitivity does not account for that cross-sectional variation once the overlap is removed. Both can hold simultaneously: the policy cycle may govern the timing of a broad slowdown without governing its incidence across industries. What the evidence no longer supports is the use of cross-industry variation to rule out an AI channel.

## 5. What this evidence does not establish: a mechanism for the Okun's Law sign flip itself

An earlier line of investigation in this project (documented in full and left unedited in the companion README for transparency) proposed a specific mechanism for why the *contemporaneous* correlation between sector output growth and the change in the sector unemployment rate flips from negative to positive during 2024-2025: that unemployment responds to a common policy-rate shock roughly 1.7 to 3 quarters faster than output does, so that in any given quarter the two variables are reflecting the policy rate from two different historical dates, and a sharp rate move desynchronizes them just enough to flip the sign of a contemporaneous regression.

That claim does not survive a proper identification check. Re-estimated with a local projection controlling for own lags and lags of the rate change, using Newey-West standard errors appropriate to the moving-average error structure a local projection at horizon h necessarily has, the peak responses for both output and unemployment are individually significant in only half of the six sector-variable combinations tested, and the *difference* between the two peak lags is not distinguishable from zero under a moving-block bootstrap (95% intervals span roughly [-15, +12] quarters at every specification and block length tried, for every sector). An earlier robustness check in this project's own prior work had reported a tight, statistically significant interval supporting the mechanism; that check used an i.i.d. bootstrap that resampled individual quarters independently, which discards the serial correlation structure quarterly macro data actually has and manufactures false precision as a result. The moving-block bootstrap, which is the standard correction for exactly this problem, reverses the conclusion.

This matters for scope, not just as a correction. Section 3's central result, that sector *employment growth* tracks the policy rate at a long, out-of-sample-validated, quantitatively accurate lag, is a single-lag relationship between one variable and a common shock, estimated with far more statistical power (a full lag-scan profile, replication across three independent multi-decade samples, and a genuine out-of-sample point prediction) than the two-lag differential this section retracts. The retraction of Section 5's claim does not weaken Section 3's; they are different statistical objects built from different variables, and only the weaker one failed.

### 5.1 Testing the mechanism's own forward predictions

The retracted mechanism had been developed into a dated forward prediction via a desynchronization index, `DESYNC_t = FFR(t-9) - FFR(t-12)`, which was said to have already passed one test (the index peaks in 2025:Q2, and all three sectors' rolling Okun correlations also peak in 2025:Q2) and to commit to two further outcomes: an unwind through 2026 and an overshoot below the pre-2022 baseline in 2027. Three checks are possible without waiting for 2027.

First, on refreshed data extending sector value added to 2026:Q1, the unwind is proceeding rather than stalling: Construction falls from +0.816 at the 2025:Q2 peak to +0.458, Manufacturing from +0.677 to +0.204, and Transportation from +0.602 to +0.279. This part of the prediction holds.

Second, the peak-date match earns much less credit than claimed. Computing the predicted peak across all 72 admissible lag pairs (any unemployment lag from 4 to 12 quarters, any longer output lag) places the peak somewhere in 2024-2025 for 90.3% of pairs and exactly in 2025:Q2 for 16.7%. The predicted peak date is governed by the shape of the 2022-2023 hiking cycle rather than by the specific lags, so a match that appeared precise was closer to a one-in-six outcome under arbitrary parameter choices.

Third, and decisively, the mechanism is a general claim about how rate shocks desynchronize output and unemployment, so it should hold in aggregate data covering nine hiking cycles rather than one. Over the full 1951-2026 aggregate sample, the correlation between DESYNC and the rolling Okun correlation is +0.091 (p = 0.139, n = 269), not significant. Disaggregated by era, it is +0.180 (p = 0.055) for 1955-1985, -0.083 (p = 0.440) for 1986-2007, and +0.602 (p < 0.0001) for 2008-2026. The relationship is present only in the window containing the episode the mechanism was constructed to explain.

The overshoot prediction fails in a way worth reporting precisely, because a partial test appears to support it. Quarters with negative DESYNC do exhibit below-baseline Okun correlations (-0.732 against a -0.667 unconditional baseline, p = 0.024). But quarters with positive DESYNC are also below baseline (-0.715), while only near-zero DESYNC sits above it (-0.505). The mechanism requires positive DESYNC to move the correlation upward, which is the entire claim about 2024-2025. A regression separating the directional from the magnitude channel, `R = a + b*DESYNC + c*|DESYNC|` with HAC standard errors, returns b = +0.0151 (p = 0.49) and c = -0.0562 (p = 0.048). The directional effect the mechanism requires is indistinguishable from zero; what the data contain is a symmetric magnitude effect in which large rate swings of either sign coincide with stronger negative Okun correlations, consistent with large rate movements clustering around recessions, when output and unemployment comove most tightly. This is a confound rather than the proposed channel.

These pooled, contemporaneous, level-on-level tests are open to a methodological objection: a mechanism about propagation should be tested dynamically, and three features of the test above are unfair to the mechanism. First, the rolling Okun correlation at quarter t is a backward-looking average over the window [t-11, t], and it was compared against a point-in-time DESYNC value. Second, the mechanism explicitly claims that a flat rate path produces no effect, so pooling in quarters where the rate path is quiet, and where the mechanism predicts nothing, biases a pooled correlation toward zero. Third, transmission lags were measured at roughly 4 quarters in 1955-1985 against 9 in the modern era, yet the modern (9, 12) pair was imposed on all seventy years.

All three corrections were applied, together with tests in first differences, a lead-lag scan, and an event study. Every correction strengthened the case against the mechanism. Matching the DESYNC measure to the correlation window reverses the sign of the estimate, from +0.091 (p = 0.139) to -0.251 (p < 0.0001, n = 258), so that greater desynchronization is associated with a *more* negative Okun correlation, opposite to the prediction. The single result that had appeared to support the mechanism, the 2008-2026 subsample correlation of +0.602, falls to -0.023 (p = 0.85) once the window is matched, and with era-appropriate lags all three eras are individually null (+0.082, -0.114, and -0.023, none significant). Tested in changes, which is the form the mechanism actually asserts, the relationship is -0.101 (p = 0.107). A lead-lag scan finds the strongest association at a four-quarter lead with a correlation of -0.362, meaning rising desynchronization forecasts a stronger, not weaker, Okun relationship. Restricting to the top tercile of |DESYNC|, the mechanism's own scope condition, yields -0.346 (p = 0.001), and within that active subsample the magnitude confound vanishes (c = -0.053, p = 0.73), leaving a wrong-signed directional effect (b = -0.103, p = 0.072).

The event study is the most direct evidence. Five historical DESYNC surges are identifiable as local maxima above the 85th percentile at least three years apart (1972:Q3, 1976:Q4, 1983:Q4, 1992:Q1, 2009:Q3). At each surge peak the rolling Okun correlation stood between -0.768 and -0.970, that is, Okun's Law holding about as tightly as it ever does, precisely when the mechanism requires the correlation to be pushed toward positive. The averaged path around these episodes shows no hump.

One caveat genuinely survives. The 2025:Q2 DESYNC value of +3.75 exceeds every historical episode, the largest being +2.48 in 1983:Q4, so the recent episode is out of sample in the magnitude of the index itself and a threshold effect switching on only above roughly +3 cannot be excluded by this data. Weighing against that possibility is the absence of any gradient in the approach to large values: the +2.48 episode produced a correlation of -0.945, among the most negative in the record. The 2022-2025 episode remains genuinely unusual in that no comparable historical desynchronization coincided with a positive Okun correlation, but the index does not explain why.

A sample-availability point belongs here as well, because it explains why the original estimates were never well determined. Quarterly real value added by industry begins in 2005, so the sector output lags rest on roughly 78 usable quarters and the first sector-level rolling Okun correlation is available only from 2008:Q4. Earlier write-ups in this project described these lags as estimated on 1991-2019 data; the 1991 sample filter binds on nothing, since the underlying series do not extend that far back. A 12-quarter lag estimated from that sample could not have been precisely identified, which is consistent with the independent finding in Section 5.

### 5.2 Comparison with the Cleveland Fed specification

Tested directly against Jacobs and Krolikowski's (2026) own reconciliation, applying their two-quarter output lag (comparing the unemployment rate to output growth two quarters earlier, with no policy-rate channel at all) to these same three sectors reduces the 2024-2025 peak Okun correlation but does not eliminate it: Construction falls from +0.82 to +0.38, Manufacturing from +0.68 to +0.60, Transportation from +0.60 to +0.25, all remaining positive. Their aggregate-level, short-lag mechanism and this paper's sector-level, long-lag rate relationship are evidently not the same phenomenon measured two ways; each captures part of the picture and neither alone accounts for the full sector-level inversion. Establishing what would is left to future work, and Section 6 below outlines what that would require.

## 6. Alternative explanations not tested here

Two candidate explanations raised in the wider 2025-2026 discourse are not addressed by any test in this paper and should be flagged rather than implicitly ruled out by omission.

**Immigration and breakeven employment.** Multiple 2025-2026 Federal Reserve publications (Dallas Fed 2025, 2026; Minneapolis Fed 2025; Kansas City Fed 2026; Murray and Vidangos 2026) document that net immigration fell sharply through 2025, with net unauthorized-immigrant flows turning negative and averaging roughly -55,000 people per month in the second half of 2025, lowering the monthly job growth needed just to hold the unemployment rate steady from roughly 100,000 in 2024 to an estimated 40,000, or lower, in 2025. This is a labor-supply story, not a labor-demand story, and it is not mutually exclusive with the rate-lag channel documented here; both could be operating simultaneously. It is a particular concern for the construction sector specifically, which draws disproportionately on immigrant and unauthorized-immigrant labor, so some part of construction's measured hiring slowdown could reflect a shrinking available workforce rather than reduced labor demand. This paper's data cannot distinguish a demand-side slowdown from a supply-side one; the prediction test in Section 3.3 shows construction hiring growth is consistent with the rate-implied path, which is not the same as showing labor supply was unconstrained over the same period. The cleaner test compares sector-level job openings and hires rates (JOLTS), which separate labor demand (openings) from realized outcomes (hires). An earlier draft of this section stated that FRED carries JOLTS detail for only two of the nine sectors examined here. That was wrong, and it was a data-collection gap rather than a data-availability one: FRED carries job openings, hires, layoffs and quits rates for all nine sectors, 307 monthly observations each from December 2000. The test has now been run and is reported in this project's `jolts_margins.py`.

It does not support a labor-supply reading of the 2024-2026 window. The relevant statistic is the fill rate, hires per job opening, which falls when firms post positions they cannot staff. In construction the fill rate did collapse, from 1.78 in 2015-2019 to 1.01 in 2023, but it has been recovering since (1.22 in 2024, 1.46 in 2025, 1.32 over the latest twelve months), and the same trough-and-recovery shape appears in leisure and hospitality (1.28 to 0.92 to 1.07) and in sectors with no particular immigrant intensity. The matching collapse is therefore located in the 2021-2023 reopening rather than in the period this paper covers, and it is unwinding through exactly the window a 2025 immigration shock would have to be tightening it. What construction shows in the latest twelve months is job openings down 1.68 percentage points from 2023 alongside layoffs 0.68 points *below* their 2015-2019 level, which is withdrawn labor demand rather than unfilled labor demand. The qualification worth keeping is that construction's fill rate remains 26 percent below its pre-pandemic level, the largest such gap of the nine sectors, so a residual supply constraint is not excluded; it is simply not what moved during the window in question.

**Tariff-driven policy uncertainty.** Business commentary through late 2025 attributed part of the hiring slowdown to firms delaying hiring decisions amid tariff policy uncertainty beginning in 2025. This operates on a much shorter timescale than the 8-9 quarter rate-lag channel documented here and would be difficult to distinguish from it using quarterly data over the sample window available, since the tariff escalation itself begins well inside the period this paper attributes to a rate shock from 2022-2023. It is noted here as an unaddressed alternative rather than tested.

## 6.05 Identified monetary policy shocks

The central limitation of Section 3 is that the federal funds rate is not exogenous: policy responds to the economy, so a reduced-form correlation between the rate and later hiring conflates the effect of policy with the conditions that prompted it. This section replaces the raw rate with published identified shock series and re-estimates the relationship as a Jordà (2005) local projection with Newey-West standard errors, controlling for four lags each of the dependent variable and the shock.

Three series reach the 2022-2023 tightening cycle: Bauer and Swanson's high-frequency surprises orthogonalized against pre-meeting macroeconomic and financial news (1988-2023), and Jarociński and Karadi's decomposition of surprises into a pure monetary policy component and a central-bank information component (1990-2024). Romer-Romer narrative shocks (1969-2019) and Nakamura-Steinsson policy news shocks (2000-2014) end too early to speak to this episode and are reported for historical reference only. Each series was verified to predict the funds rate rising, confirming a contractionary-positive sign convention rather than assuming it.

The horizon tested is pre-specified at eight quarters, matching the claim in Section 3, rather than selected as the maximum of an estimated response profile. Coefficients are scaled to one standard deviation of each shock.

| Sector | Bauer-Swanson | JK pure policy | JK information |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Construction | -1.11 (t = -1.8) | +0.27 | +0.43 (t = 0.7) |
| Manufacturing | -0.54 (t = -1.6) | +0.30 | +0.82 (t = 2.1) |
| Transportation | -0.46 (t = -1.1) | +0.31 | +0.82 (t = 2.4) |
| Education and health (control) | +0.17 (t = 0.6) | +0.13 | -0.01 (t = -0.1) |

Using the most thoroughly purged series, the contractionary sign appears in all three goods sectors at the predicted horizon, and the control sector shows no response on any series. This is the strongest causal evidence in the paper. It is nonetheless not statistically significant at conventional levels in any goods sector (Construction is closest, t = -1.9 at nine quarters), and the Jarociński-Karadi pure policy shock returns small positive coefficients that neither corroborate nor contradict. With roughly 130 quarterly observations and noisy shock measures, this test is underpowered; the appropriate conclusion is that the sector hiring response is directionally consistent with an identified contractionary channel at the predicted horizon, and is not established at conventional significance.

A further result carries more weight than the sign test itself. The central-bank information shock is significantly positive in manufacturing and transportation, which is what theory predicts for it: a signal that the economy is stronger than markets believed should raise employment in cyclically exposed sectors. Two channels therefore operate with opposite signs, and any correlation computed against the observed policy rate sums them. This provides a specific mechanical reason, independent of the inference problems in Section 6.1, why the reduced-form estimates in Section 3 cannot be interpreted causally, and it explains the wrong-signed local projections on the raw rate change reported in Section 5.

Finally, the retracted differential-timing mechanism does not reappear under identification. Across six sector-shock combinations the estimated gap between the output and unemployment response peaks is -10, -4, 0, -5, 0 and 0 quarters, and in none of the six is either peak individually significant. The retraction in Section 5 stands.

## 6.1 Audit of this paper's own inference

An adversarial audit of the five weakest points in this analysis produced four corrections that are incorporated above and reported here in one place, because they bound what the paper is entitled to claim.

**Statistical inference throughout this project's underlying code used `scipy.stats.pearsonr`, whose i.i.d. assumption fails here.** Growth rates are 4-quarter overlapping differences and the underlying series are highly persistent, so consecutive observations are mechanically correlated. Reported magnitudes are unaffected; nominal significance levels were overstated everywhere they appeared. All significance claims retained in this paper have been recomputed with Newey-West standard errors and a circular-shift bootstrap.

**The core relationship survives that recomputation on its own sample.** The nine-sector average hiring growth against the funds rate lagged nine quarters, on 2006-2026 data excluding COVID quarters (n = 75), gives r = -0.741 with a HAC p-value of 0.0006, a circular-shift bootstrap p-value below 0.0001, and a coefficient that remains significant (p = 0.012) after controlling for four lags of the dependent variable. That last test matters because the standard objection to a long-lag rate correlation is that rate peaks precede recessions, so the association could reflect business-cycle periodicity rather than a transmission channel. It survives.

**It does not generalise to the longer record.** On the full available history (n = 135) the same correlation is -0.368, and it survives neither the bootstrap (p = 0.105) nor the own-lag controls (p = 0.542). This is consistent with the independently measured lengthening of the transmission lag from roughly four quarters before 1986 to roughly nine after, and it means the claim in this paper is properly a claim about the post-2006 US economy rather than a general one. It also means the 2006-onward window contains the 2022-2025 episode being explained, so the out-of-sample figure of -0.37 is the appropriate number for validation purposes, and the in-sample -0.74 should not be quoted as though it were validated.

**The prediction exercise and the AI test were both overstated**, as recorded in the abstract and Sections 3.3 and 4. The prediction residuals are uninformative at the precision originally claimed, and the nine-sector AI test lacks the power to distinguish no effect from a moderate one.

## 7. Limitations

- **Correlation and a validated lag are not a structural model.** The out-of-sample replication and the quantitative prediction test in Section 3 are considerably more demanding than a simple in-sample correlation, but neither is a structural VAR with a credibly identified monetary policy shock. A skeptical reader is entitled to want that before accepting the channel as causal rather than merely consistent.
- **The lag is era-dependent, not a structural constant** (4 quarters pre-1986, 8-9 quarters after), so "monetary policy takes about two years to reach hiring in these sectors" is a claim about the post-1986 economy specifically.
- **The prediction test uses a single regressor.** Landing within 0.01 percentage points for construction is a striking result and is reported honestly, but a one-variable model that happens to land that close is also partly favorable luck; the defensible claim is that these sectors are close to their rate-implied path, not that the model is precise to a hundredth of a point.
- **This paper does not establish why the sector-level Okun's Law correlation itself flips sign**, only that the associated hiring slowdown's magnitude and timing are consistent with the policy rate. Section 5 documents that a specific attempt to close that gap failed.
- **The immigration channel is now tested on one margin only, and the tariff channel not at all**, as detailed in Section 6. The JOLTS fill-rate evidence places the matching collapse in the 2021-2023 reopening rather than in this paper's window, which weighs against a labor-supply reading of 2024-2026, but a fill rate is one margin and not a labor-supply model. Construction's fill rate remains well below its pre-pandemic level, and nothing here rules out a supply constraint operating alongside the rate channel rather than instead of it.
- **n is modest.** The historical out-of-sample validation has 136 quarterly observations; the in-sample test excluding COVID has 75. Both are adequate for the correlations reported but not large by the standards of modern applied macro.

## 8. Conclusion

The 2022-2025 hiring slowdown in construction and manufacturing, the two most historically rate-sensitive of the sectors examined here, is quantitatively close to what an out-of-sample-validated, decades-old relationship between the federal funds rate and sector hiring would have predicted using only the realized path of interest rates. AI exposure does not predict which sectors slowed, and the one sector with no historical rate sensitivity is the one sector that did not slow. That combination of evidence weighs against reading the physical-sector hiring slowdown as new evidence of AI-driven labor displacement, and is offered as a narrow, sector-level complement to Jacobs and Krolikowski's (2026) aggregate reconciliation of the same broader puzzle, using a different mechanism (a long policy-rate lag rather than a short output lag) that this paper shows only partially overlaps with theirs when tested directly on the same sectors. A companion claim from earlier work in this project, that a differential response lag between output and unemployment explains why the sector-level Okun's Law correlation itself inverts, does not survive proper identification and is retracted here rather than carried forward.
